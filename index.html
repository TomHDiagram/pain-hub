How to use Pain Hub

Pain Hub is for people living with arthritis pain in New Zealand. It gives you practical, trusted tips from Arthritis New Zealand that you can read and use straight away.

You can simply read the tips. If you would like tips that fit your own situation, you can also use an AI tool such as ChatGPT, Gemini or Claude to talk through what your pain is getting in the way of. The prompt on this page asks the AI tool to use only the trusted advice on this page.

Pain Hub is also built so that AI tools can read and understand it, which is why part of this page is written for them.

The page has three sections:

  1. Top tips for managing pain and arthritis – for you to read any time
  2. How to use AI to make these tips relevant to your situation – if you want it
  3. The ‘database’ of content that makes Pain Hub work – mainly for AI tools

Understanding what pain is (and isn't) telling you

  • More pain doesn't always mean more damage — persistent pain often reflects a sensitive nervous system rather than ongoing injury
  • Pain, stress, poor sleep and low mood can all turn the volume up on how much pain you feel, without anything new being wrong in the joint
  • Understanding this can take the fear out of movement, and is often the first shift that makes everything else easier

Pain is your body's alarm system. It exists to protect you, which is why a sudden injury hurts. When pain carries on for a long time (doctors usually say more than three months), the nerve pathways can become more sensitive. The alarm keeps going off even when there is no new damage. That doesn't make the pain any less real. It means your nervous system is being over-protective.

This helps explain why the amount of pain you feel doesn't always match what a scan or X-ray shows. It also explains why poor sleep, stress, worry, low mood or feeling you have no control can make pain feel worse, and why living with pain can in turn affect your sleep and mood.

Many people find that understanding this takes some of the fear out of moving and doing everyday things. There is no one-size-fits-all approach to pain, and medication is only one part of the picture.

Keep moving your joints – even when it's hardMovement and exercise help reduce pain, improve flexibility, strengthen joints, and boost overall wellbeing.Read more

Keeping moving, gently

  • Regular gentle movement reduces stiffness and helps maintain strength and function
  • Start small and build up gradually — a few minutes at a time is enough to begin
  • Movement also supports sleep, mood and energy, not just the joint itself

Joints are made to move. When you move less, joints can get stiffer and the muscles that support them can get weaker, which can make pain and tiredness harder to live with. Regular gentle movement helps keep your joints, muscles and function in better shape, and many people find it helps their sleep, mood and energy too.

It is common to worry that moving will make things worse, or to feel too tired, too sore or unsure where to start. Those feelings make sense. Movement doesn't have to be hard or long to be worthwhile. A few minutes at a time counts, and there is no single right type. The best activity is one you enjoy enough to keep doing.

Some muscle soreness after trying something new is normal and usually settles within a day or two. If pain lingers or gets worse, or you feel worse after each time, it's a sign to adjust and talk with your GP or physiotherapist. Arthritis Assist can also help you find local classes and people to get started with.

Sore from yesterday's activities? Learn to pace yourself so you don't overdo itPacing can help you balance activity and rest, manage your energy and keep doing the things that matter to you.Read more

Pacing yourself

  • Break activities into smaller, manageable chunks
  • Take breaks before you need them, not after
  • Aim for a similar amount on good days and bad days, rather than doing too much on a good day and paying for it afterwards

Many people with arthritis know this pattern. On a good day you get a lot done. Later that day or the next, you're sorer, stiffer or more tired, and you spend time recovering. Once you feel better, you do a lot again. This is sometimes called the boom and bust cycle.

Pacing is a way of getting off that rollercoaster. It isn't about avoiding activity or giving up things you enjoy. It's about finding an amount you can keep up, rather than pushing until pain or tiredness makes you stop. Some people think of it as protecting tomorrow.

Pacing can mean splitting a job into smaller parts, taking breaks before you need them, swapping between harder and easier tasks, and spreading big jobs across the week. There is no single right amount for everyone, so expect some trial and error. A good way to start is to pick one activity that often leaves you paying for it later, change one thing about how you do it, then notice how you feel that day and the next.

Adapting the activity rather than giving it up

  • Change how, where or for how long you do something
  • Keep the parts of it that matter most to you
  • Adapting isn't giving up — it's how you keep doing what you value

When pain gets in the way of something you love, it's easy to stop doing it altogether. Over time that can wear down your confidence and make daily life feel smaller. Adapting is a middle path. You keep what the activity means to you, and change how it fits around your pain.

That might mean doing it at a different time of day or in a different place, doing it for less time, or doing part of it rather than all of it. What matters is the reason you do it, whether that's time with a person, a place, or a routine that helps you feel like yourself.

Arthritis NZ describes acceptance as the first step in managing pain. That isn't giving up. It means recognising that you can take an active part in managing your pain. Because everyone responds differently, it can take a few tries to find what works for you. Pain may limit some things, but it doesn't have to control your life.

Protecting your joints

  • Think about your posture and positioning during a task
  • Avoid staying in one position for too long
  • Use your larger, stronger joints for heavy work and reduce unnecessary strain

Your joints cope with a lot through the day, and some tasks put the same joints under strain again and again. Protecting your joints starts with noticing which tasks do that, and then finding ways to share the load around.

That can mean changing position more often so you're not held in one place for too long, breaking a task into parts so no single position is held for long, or asking someone else to do the heaviest part. It's also worth thinking about which jobs really need to be done in one go.

How to protect your joints depends on which joints are affected and what you do each day, so it's worth getting advice that fits you. An occupational therapist or physiotherapist can show you what suits you for a particular task, including how to position your body and hands. Your GP can refer you, and Arthritis Assist can point you in the right direction. The Arthritis NZ page below has more everyday ideas for looking after your joints.

Everyday tasks hard to grip, reach or balance? Tools and supports can helpSupportive footwear, walking sticks, raised chairs and larger-handled utensils can all reduce strain.Read more

Using tools and supports to make things easier

  • Supportive footwear, walking sticks, raised chairs and larger-handled utensils can all reduce strain
  • The right aid can be the difference between managing something and giving it up
  • An occupational therapist can help work out what would suit your situation

Arthritis can make everyday tasks harder or sore, whether that's opening a jar, getting dressed, reaching for something, getting out of a chair or keeping your balance. Tools and aids can take some of the force off your hands, feet and other joints, and help you stay independent and keep doing what matters to you.

There are aids for many kinds of tasks, from the kitchen and bathroom to dressing and getting around. What works for one person may not suit another, which is why an occupational therapist (OT) can be so helpful. They can look at what you want to do, work out what's getting in the way, and suggest or adapt equipment that fits you.

You can ask your GP about a referral to a hospital occupational therapy service for funded equipment, look for an OT yourself, or buy tools from a mobility store. The Arthritis NZ page below explains where to look, and Arthritis Assist is free to call if you'd like to talk it through.

Too much to do and not enough energy? Plan and prioritiseDecide what really matters to you, and let the rest wait.Read more

Planning and prioritising your energy

  • Decide what really matters to you, and let the rest wait
  • Spread jobs across the day or the week rather than doing them all at once
  • Break big jobs into smaller ones, and ask for help with the things that don't need to be done by you

When you live with pain, your energy is limited, and some days there is even less of it. Trying to do everything often leads to overdoing it, then paying for it later. Planning and prioritising means deciding ahead of time where your energy goes.

Start with what matters most to you, whether that's a person, a role or a routine, and give that your energy first. Other jobs can wait, be shared out, or be left undone. Spreading jobs across the day or the week, and breaking big jobs into smaller steps, can make a full list feel more manageable.

Asking for help is a sensible use of your energy, not a weakness. So is saying no sometimes. Arthritis NZ describes planning, setting priorities and pacing as working together to reduce stress and help you keep going. You don't have to get the plan right the first time. Adjust it as you learn what works for you.

Caught out by flare-ups? Have a plan readyEase back and rest – a flare is temporary.Read more

Having a plan for flare-ups

  • Ease back and rest — a flare is temporary
  • Prioritise only the essentials while it lasts
  • Return to your normal activity gradually as things settle

A flare-up is a temporary worsening of your symptoms. It can bring more pain, stiffness, swelling and tiredness, and it can happen even when you're doing everything right. Flares are often linked to doing too much or to pressure from other people, and they can follow things like illness too. They are usually temporary. Arthritis NZ suggests speaking with your doctor if your symptoms last.

You can't always prevent a flare-up, but you can be ready for one. A plan made when you're feeling better takes a lot of pressure off when you're not. Think about what you would let go of, who you would tell, and who could help. Being kind to yourself matters too. A flare-up isn't your fault.

When things settle, ease back into your usual activities gradually rather than trying to catch up all at once. If you're in a flare right now and finding it hard, Arthritis Assist is free to call on 0800 663 463.

Using relaxation and attention techniques

  • Deep breathing, relaxation and mindfulness help your body release tension it's holding
  • Deliberately focusing on something absorbing and enjoyable is a recognised pain strategy, not just a distraction
  • These reduce the impact pain has on you, even when the pain itself doesn't change

Pain isn't only in the joints. Worry, tension and frustration can build up around it, and stress can make pain feel worse. Relaxation, mindfulness and absorbing activities are ways of changing how you relate to pain, rather than ways of making it go away.

Mindfulness means noticing your thoughts, feelings and body sensations in the present moment, with kindness and curiosity, rather than judging them. It doesn't mean emptying your mind, and it doesn't replace medical treatment. Many people find it helps them feel calmer and less overwhelmed by the emotional load of pain. Giving your attention to something you find absorbing and enjoyable can have a similar effect.

It takes practice, and feeling awkward at first is normal. The Arthritis NZ page below has more on mindfulness to explore. If you're not sure whether it suits you, talk with your GP or another health provider.

Looking after the things that influence pain

  • Sleep, stress, mood and nutrition all affect how much pain you feel
  • Pain management isn't only about the painful joint itself
  • Improving one of these often makes the others easier

Pain management isn't only about the sore joint. Sleep, stress, mood and what you eat can all change how much pain you feel, and living with pain can in turn disturb your sleep and lower your mood. This can become a cycle that feels endless, but it can be broken at any point.

Because these things are connected, a small improvement in one area can make the others easier. Arthritis NZ's guidance on mental health and pain treats looking after your mind as part of looking after your body. It points to things like mindfulness, gentle movement, better sleep, talking to someone you trust and keeping a gentle daily routine.

Everyone's needs are different, and you don't have to fix everything at once. Noticing which of these is affecting you most can be a good starting point for a conversation with your GP. Arthritis Assist is free to call if you'd like to talk it through.

More: Mental health and pain, Arthritis NZ

Feeling on your own with this? Get supportTalk with your GP, pharmacist, physiotherapist or occupational therapist.Read more

Getting support rather than managing everything alone

  • Talk with your GP, pharmacist, physiotherapist or occupational therapist
  • Prepare for that conversation — track your symptoms, write down your questions, and be specific about what's changed
  • Arthritis Assist is free to call on 0800 663 463

You don't have to manage arthritis pain on your own. Living with pain can feel isolating, especially if you have felt unheard or aren't sure who to ask. Arthritis NZ describes building a team of people who understand what you're going through and want to help you live well as an important part of managing pain.

Your team might include your GP, pharmacist, physiotherapist or occupational therapist, along with friends and whānau who understand. Each can help with different things, so it's fine to ask who is best placed to help with the question you have.

Appointments can be short, so a bit of preparation helps. Keep track of your symptoms, including what has changed and when. Write down your questions beforehand, and be specific about what you want to be able to do. Arthritis Assist is free to call on 0800 663 463 and can give advice, resources and information on every type of arthritis. Your feelings are valid, and support is available.

2How to use AI to make these tips relevant to you

The great thing about AI tools like ChatGPT is that you can talk to them and ask questions. The challenge is getting them to give you reliable information.

This is why we have created Pain Hub in a way that improves the quality of the advice provided by your AI tool.

To make the tips on this page relevant to you, paste this prompt into ChatGPT, Gemini or Claude (or any AI tool).

Please don’t type your name or other identifying details into an AI tool. Read more about privacy.

Pain Hub prompt
You are helping someone in New Zealand who lives with pain from arthritis.

Use ONLY the advice on this Arthritis New Zealand Pain Hub page: https://tomhdiagram.github.io/pain-hub/
Open and read the whole page first, including the tables at the bottom, and follow the "Guidance for the AI" in them. If you cannot open the page, say so and stop. Do not answer from other sources.

1. Ask me what my pain is getting in the way of. Ask one question at a time. Do not ask for my name or any identifying details.
2. If anything I say matches a Safety or Redirect message on the page, give me that message first.
3. Otherwise choose the one tip on the page that best fits what I told you. Show me the tip's wording as it is written, then help me apply it to my situation, following the guidance for that tip.
4. Give me the link for the tip.
5. If the page does not cover what I need, say so and suggest I call Arthritis Assist on 0800 663 463.
How does the prompt work?

The prompt tells the AI tool to ask you some questions, and then it tells the AI tool to only use trusted Pain Hub advice when answering.

It points the AI tool to this page, including the tables in section 3, which say which tip suits which situation and what the AI tool may and may not do.

Rules for every tip

  • The LLM may only rearrange or rescale what the person has already told it: how much, how long, how often, in what order, with what breaks, with whose help.
  • The LLM must never introduce a new activity, exercise, stretch, body position, posture or product that the person did not mention.
  • Where the natural application would need a new activity, position or product, the LLM must instead suggest asking a physiotherapist, occupational therapist, pharmacist or GP.
  • No medical claims, no diagnosis, no promises about outcomes.
  • No financial, legal or employment advice, and no naming of counselling or crisis services — those are handled by Redirect and Safety messages, never generated.
  • Framing and Situation are plain New Zealand English, second person, warm but not gushing, no exclamation marks.
  • Individual principle rows in Resources may add stricter guardrails on top of these. A principle-specific guardrail can only narrow what is allowed, never widen it.

Disclaimer and privacy

We (Arthritis New Zealand) provide clinically reviewed and trusted advice. We do not control how AI tools behave. As such, while we have made every effort to improve the quality of advice provided by AI tools, we can’t guarantee that the AI tools will get it right every time. They make mistakes. We encourage you to always check the source information that we provide.

We understand that AI can help people get health advice, but we recommend that you do not enter personally identifiable details (like people’s names) into free AI tools like Google AI overviews or ChatGPT. It is unclear how these tools may use your personal details in the future.
